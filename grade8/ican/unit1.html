NC.8.NS.1 — Rational and irrational numbersPriority

NS.1-1Approaching (3)

I can recognize that every number has a decimal expansion and describe what that expansion looks like for rational and irrational numbers.

Try thisWrite each number as a decimal: 5/8, 4/11, and √3 (a calculator shows √3 = 1.7320508…). Then describe each decimal as terminating, repeating, or non-repeating and non-terminating.
Show answer
Answer: 5/8 = 0.625 (terminates); 4/11 = 0.3636… (repeats); √3 = 1.7320508… (never ends, never repeats)
  1. 5 ÷ 8 = 0.625 — the division stops, so it terminates.
  2. 4 ÷ 11 = 0.363636… — the block 36 repeats forever.
  3. √3 = 1.7320508… — the digits never end and no block repeats.
  4. Terminating and repeating decimals are rational; √3 is irrational.

NS.1-8Mastery (5)

I can build and defend a model or graphic organizer of the real number system that shows how natural, whole, integer, rational, and irrational numbers are related.

Try thisDescribe a nested diagram (graphic organizer) of the real number system using words. Place one number that belongs ONLY in each region: natural; whole but not natural; integer but not whole; rational but not integer; irrational. Then defend your model: why is every natural number also an integer, and why is the irrational set drawn outside the rational set?
Show answer
Answer: Real numbers ⊃ Rational ⊃ Integers ⊃ Whole ⊃ Natural, with Irrational beside Rational; e.g. 4, 0, −3, ⅔, √2
  1. Model: a big Real Numbers box holding two parts — Rational and Irrational. Inside Rational: Integers ⊃ Whole ⊃ Natural (each circle inside the next).
  2. Examples: natural 4; whole only 0; integer only −3; rational only ⅔ (0.666…); irrational √2.
  3. Every natural number is also an integer because integers are the whole numbers plus their negatives, and the whole numbers include all counting numbers — so the Natural circle sits inside the Integer circle.
  4. Irrational sits outside Rational because a number either can be written as a fraction of integers (rational) or cannot (irrational) — it can never be both.
  5. Together, Rational and Irrational make up all the real numbers.

NS.1-9Mastery (5)

I can explain why a number written in fractional form, such as 3π/4, can still be irrational, and justify my reasoning.

Try thisDevon says, "2π/5 is rational because it is written as a fraction." Explain why Devon is wrong, and compare 2π/5 to 2/5.
Show answer
Answer: 2π/5 is irrational: π is irrational, and multiplying or dividing an irrational number by a nonzero rational number (here 2 and 5) keeps it irrational. 2/5 = 0.4 is a ratio of two integers, so it is rational
  1. Rational means it can be written as a fraction of two INTEGERS.
  2. 2/5 has integers on top and bottom, so 2/5 = 0.4 is rational.
  3. In 2π/5 the top is 2π, which is irrational (not an integer), so this fraction is NOT a ratio of two integers — and it cannot be rewritten as one.
  4. Multiplying or dividing an irrational number by a non-zero integer keeps it irrational: 2π/5 ≈ 1.2566370…, never ending or repeating.
  5. So looking like a fraction is not enough — 2π/5 is irrational.

NS.2-8Mastery (5)

Try thisUse the estimation algorithm to estimate √33 to the nearest tenth. Explain why each step works, then defend the accuracy of your answer by squaring.
Show answer
Answer: √33 ≈ 5.7
  1. Trap it: 25 < 33 < 36, so √33 is between 5 and 6 — the root must sit between the roots of the surrounding perfect squares.
  2. Fraction: (33 − 25) ÷ (36 − 25) = 8 ÷ 11 ≈ 0.73 — 33 is 8 of the 11 steps from 25 to 36, so √33 is about that far from 5 to 6.
  3. Add: 5 + 0.73 = 5.73 → about 5.7.
  4. Defend: 5.7² = 32.49 and 5.8² = 33.64, so √33 is between 5.7 and 5.8. The midpoint 5.75² = 33.0625 > 33, so √33 < 5.75 and rounds to 5.7.
  5. Calculator check: √33 = 5.744… → 5.7 ✓
