Part 1 · NC.8.G.5 · Unit 4

Angle Relationships

Big idea

When angles share a side or two lines cross, the angles are tied together. If you know one, you can find the others.

Words to know

Adjacent anglesTwo angles that share a vertex and a side. They sit side by side.
Vertical anglesAngles across from each other where two lines cross. They are equal.
Linear pairTwo adjacent angles that make a straight line. They add to 180°.
ComplementaryTwo angles that add to 90°. Together they make a right angle.
SupplementaryTwo angles that add to 180°. Together they make a straight line.
Right angleAn angle of exactly 90°. A small square marks it.

Look at how the angles touch. That tells you the rule.

RelationshipWhat it looks likeRule
Vertical anglesAcross from each other, like an Xequal
Linear pairSide by side on a straight lineadd to 180°
SupplementaryTwo angles that make a straight angleadd to 180°
ComplementaryTwo angles that fill a right angleadd to 90°
Memory tip: Complementary comes before Supplementary in the alphabet, and 90 comes before 180.
Example 1 · Two lines cross

Two lines cross. ∠1 = 58°. Find ∠2, ∠3 and ∠4.

  • ∠3 is across from ∠1. Vertical angles are equal, so ∠3 = 58°.
  • ∠1 and ∠2 make a straight line. 180 − 58 = 122, so ∠2 = 122°.
  • ∠4 is across from ∠2, so ∠4 = 122°.

Check: 58 + 122 + 58 + 122 = 360. All the way around is 360°.

Example 2 · Solve for x

The two angles fill a right angle. Find x and each angle.

  • They fill a right angle, so they are complementary. Write: (x + 20) + (2x + 10) = 90
  • Combine like terms: 3x + 30 = 90
  • Subtract 30: 3x = 60. Divide by 3: x = 20
  • Plug in: x + 20 = 40° and 2x + 10 = 50°. Check: 40 + 50 = 90 ✓
Try it · Turn a line

Move the slider to turn one line. Watch all four angles. Which ones always match? Which ones always add to 180°?

Skills you need
  • Name a pair as vertical, linear pair, complementary or supplementary.
  • Use the rule to find a missing angle.
  • Write and solve an equation to find x.
  • Plug x back in to find each angle.
Common mistakes
  • Mistake: Setting vertical angles to add to 180°.Fix: Vertical angles are across the X. They are equal.
  • Mistake: Using 180° for complementary angles.Fix: Complementary = 90° (a right angle). Supplementary = 180°.
  • Mistake: Stopping at x.Fix: If the question asks for the angle, plug x back in.
Part 2 · NC.8.G.5 · Unit 4

Parallel Lines Cut by a Transversal

Big idea

When one line crosses two parallel lines, it makes 8 angles. They come in only two sizes. Some pairs are equal. Some pairs add to 180°.

Words to know

Parallel linesLines that never meet. Little arrows (>) on the lines show they are parallel. We write m ∥ n.
TransversalA line that crosses two or more lines. We call it t.
Interior anglesAngles between the two parallel lines.
Exterior anglesAngles outside the two parallel lines.
AlternateOn opposite sides of the transversal.
CongruentThe same size. Congruent angles have the same number of degrees.

First find where the two angles sit. That tells you the rule.

PairWhere they sitRule
Corresponding
∠1 & ∠5, ∠2 & ∠6, ∠3 & ∠7, ∠4 & ∠8		Same corner at each crossingequal
Alternate interior
∠3 & ∠6, ∠4 & ∠5		Between the lines, opposite sides of tequal
Alternate exterior
∠1 & ∠8, ∠2 & ∠7		Outside the lines, opposite sides of tequal
Same-side interior
∠3 & ∠5, ∠4 & ∠6		Between the lines, same side of tadd to 180°
Quick check with your eyes: If both angles look the same size, they are equal. If one is sharp (small) and one is wide (big), they add to 180°. The rules only work when the lines are parallel.
Example 1 · Use the rules

Lines m and n are parallel. ∠2 = 65°. Find ∠6, ∠3 and ∠5.

  • ∠6 is in the same corner as ∠2 at the other crossing. Corresponding angles are equal, so ∠6 = 65°.
  • ∠3 is across the X from ∠2. Vertical angles are equal, so ∠3 = 65°.
  • ∠3 and ∠5 are between the lines, on the same side of t. Same-side interior angles add to 180°. 180 − 65 = 115, so ∠5 = 115°.

Check: ∠5 and ∠6 make a straight line. 115 + 65 = 180 ✓

Example 2 · Solve for x

Lines m and n are parallel. ∠3 = (3x + 15)° and ∠6 = (5x − 7)°. Find x and the angle.

  • The angles are between the lines, on opposite sides of t. They are alternate interior angles, so they are equal: 3x + 15 = 5x − 7
  • Subtract 3x from both sides: 15 = 2x − 7
  • Add 7: 22 = 2x. Divide by 2: x = 11
  • Plug in: 3(11) + 15 = 48°. Check: 5(11) − 7 = 48 ✓
Try it · Tilt the transversal

Move the slider to tilt line t. Tap a pair name to light up two angles. Are they equal, or do they add to 180°?

Skills you need
  • Find where each angle sits: between or outside the lines, and which side of t.
  • Name the pair: corresponding, alternate interior, alternate exterior or same-side interior.
  • Decide: equal, or add to 180°.
  • Write and solve an equation. Plug x back in.
Common mistakes
  • Mistake: Setting same-side interior angles equal.Fix: Same-side interior angles add to 180°. One is small and one is big.
  • Mistake: Mixing up alternate and same-side.Fix: Alternate = opposite sides of t. Same-side = the same side of t.
  • Mistake: Using these rules when the lines are not parallel.Fix: Look for the arrows or the ∥ sign first.

Triangle Angle Sum and the Exterior Angle Theorem

Big idea

The three angles inside any triangle add to 180°. An outside angle equals the two far inside angles added together.

Words to know

Interior angleAn angle inside the triangle. Every triangle has 3.
Triangle Angle SumThe 3 interior angles always add to 180°.
Exterior angleAn angle outside the triangle. You make it by extending one side past a corner.
Remote interior anglesThe two inside angles that are far from the exterior angle. They do not touch it.
Adjacent interior angleThe inside angle right next to the exterior angle. The two make a straight line (180°).
Exterior Angle TheoremExterior angle = the sum of the two remote interior angles.
TheoremRuleExample
Triangle Angle Sum∠A + ∠B + ∠C = 180°x + 72 + 55 = 180, so x = 53
Exterior Angleoutside = far + farx = 35 + 70 = 105
Why 180°? Tear the 3 corners off a paper triangle. Put them side by side. They make a straight line, and a straight line is 180°.
Example 1 · Missing angle

Find x.

  • The angles of a triangle add to 180°. Write: x + 72 + 55 = 180
  • Add the numbers you know: x + 127 = 180
  • Subtract 127: x = 53

Check: 53 + 72 + 55 = 180 ✓

Example 2 · Exterior angle with x

Find x. Then find the exterior angle, (3x + 10)°.

  • The exterior angle equals the two far inside angles added: 3x + 10 = 50 + (x + 20)
  • Simplify the right side: 3x + 10 = x + 70
  • Subtract x: 2x + 10 = 70. Subtract 10: 2x = 60. So x = 30
  • Exterior angle: 3(30) + 10 = 100°. Check: 50 + (30 + 20) = 100 ✓
Try it · Change the triangle

Move the sliders to change ∠A and ∠B. Watch ∠C and the outside angle at B. What is always true?

Skills you need
  • Add the angles you know. Subtract from 180°.
  • Find the two inside angles that are far from the outside angle.
  • Write an equation with x. Combine like terms and solve.
  • Plug x back in to find the angle.
Common mistakes
  • Mistake: Subtracting the far angles to get the exterior angle.Fix: The exterior angle is the SUM: far + far.
  • Mistake: Using the angle right next to the exterior angle.Fix: Use only the two inside angles that do not touch it.
  • Mistake: Putting the exterior angle into the 180° sum.Fix: Only the 3 inside angles add to 180°.
Part 4 · NC.8.G.5 · Unit 4

Angle-Angle (AA) Similarity

Big idea

If two angles of one triangle match two angles of another, the triangles are similar. They have the same shape. Every side grows by the same scale factor.

Words to know

Similar (~)Same shape, but maybe a different size. All the angles match.
Corresponding partsParts in the same spot on each triangle. They match.
AA similarityIf two angles of one triangle equal two angles of another, the triangles are similar.
Scale factorThe number you multiply every side by. Find it with new side ÷ matching old side.
ProportionalAll sides grow (or shrink) by the same scale factor.
Similarity statement△ABC ~ △DEF. The order shows the matches: A ↔ D, B ↔ E, C ↔ F.
StepWhat to do
1Find any missing third angle: 180 − one angle − the other angle.
2Do two pairs of angles match? Then the triangles are similar (AA).
3Scale factor = big side ÷ the matching small side.
4Multiply the other small side by the scale factor to find the missing side.
Why are two angles enough? The angles add to 180°. If two pairs match, the third pair has to match too.
Watch out: Similar triangles multiply by the scale factor. They do not add.
Example 1 · Are they similar?

△ABC has ∠A = 50° and ∠B = 60°. △DEF has ∠D = 50° and ∠E = 60°. Are they similar?

  • ∠A = ∠D = 50°. That is one pair.
  • ∠B = ∠E = 60°. That is a second pair.
  • Two pairs match, so yes. By AA, △ABC ~ △DEF.

The third angles match too: 180 − 50 − 60 = 70° in both.

Example 2 · Missing side

△ABC ~ △DEF. Side 6 matches side 9. Side 8 matches side x. Find x.

  • Use the pair you know both of: 6 and 9.
  • Scale factor = 9 ÷ 6 = 1.5.
  • Multiply the other side: x = 8 × 1.5 = 12.

Check: 12 ÷ 8 = 1.5 ✓ The same scale factor.

Try it · Grow or shrink a triangle

Move the slider to change the scale factor. Watch the sides and the angles. What changes? What stays the same?

Skills you need
  • Find a third angle with 180 − one − other.
  • Check for two matching pairs of angles.
  • Match sides by their spot in the triangle.
  • Find the scale factor and multiply.
Common mistakes
  • Mistake: Saying “each triangle shows two angles, so they are similar.”Fix: The two angles must match each other. Find the third angles and compare.
  • Mistake: Adding to find a side (6 → 9 is +3, so 8 → 11).Fix: Multiply by the scale factor: 8 × 1.5 = 12.
  • Mistake: Matching the wrong sides.Fix: Match sides by the angles they sit between.

Legs, Hypotenuse, and the Squares on the Sides

Big idea

In a right triangle, the two small squares on the legs fill the big square on the hypotenuse exactly: a² + b² = c².

Words to know

Right triangleA triangle with one 90° angle. A small square marks it.
Legs (a and b)The two sides that make the right angle.
Hypotenuse (c)The longest side. It is across from the right angle.
SquaredA number times itself. 5² = 5 × 5 = 25. (Not 5 × 2.)
Square root (√)Undoes squaring. √25 = 5, because 5 × 5 = 25.
Pythagorean Theorema² + b² = c². It works only on right triangles.
a² + b² = c²
a and b are the legs. c is the hypotenuse. The hypotenuse never touches the little square. It sits across from it.

Squares to know

3² = 94² = 165² = 256² = 367² = 498² = 649² = 8110² = 10011² = 12112² = 14413² = 16915² = 225
Example 1 · Squares on the sides

A right triangle has legs 6 and 8 and hypotenuse 10. Squares are built on each side. Compare the areas.

  • Square on the leg 6: 6² = 36.
  • Square on the leg 8: 8² = 64.
  • Add the leg squares: 36 + 64 = 100.
  • Square on the hypotenuse: 10² = 100. They match ✓
Example 2 · Which side is c?

In △PQR the right angle is at Q. PQ = 9, QR = 40 and PR = 41. Write the theorem.

  • The hypotenuse is across from the right angle at Q. That is PR = 41.
  • The legs touch Q: PQ = 9 and QR = 40.
  • Legs on the left, hypotenuse alone on the right: 9² + 40² = 41²
  • Check: 81 + 1,600 = 1,681, and 41² = 1,681 ✓
Try it · Build the squares

Move the sliders to change the legs. Add the two small squares. Compare with the big square.

Skills you need
  • Find the right angle. Name the hypotenuse across from it.
  • Square a number: multiply it by itself.
  • Write a² + b² = c² with the legs on the left.
  • Know the theorem works only on right triangles.
Common mistakes
  • Mistake: Thinking 5² means 5 × 2 = 10.Fix: 5² = 5 × 5 = 25.
  • Mistake: Putting the hypotenuse with a leg on the left side.Fix: c is the longest side. It goes alone: a² + b² = c².
  • Mistake: Using a² + b² = c² on a triangle with no right angle.Fix: Look for the 90° mark first.
Part 6 · NC.8.G.7 · Unit 5

Find the Hypotenuse or a Missing Leg

Big idea

Know both legs? Add the squares. Know the hypotenuse and one leg? Subtract the squares. Then take the square root.

Words to know

Perfect squareA number that is a whole number times itself, like 49 = 7 × 7. Its square root is a whole number.
Exact answerThe answer with the √ sign, like √65.
≈Means “is about.” √65 ≈ 8.1.
Nearest tenthOne digit after the decimal point. Look at the next digit. 5 or more: round up. 4 or less: keep it.
You knowDo thisFormula
Both legsADD the squaresc = √(a² + b²)
The hypotenuse and one legSUBTRACT the squaresa = √(c² − b²)
Check your answer: The hypotenuse is always the longest side. A leg must be shorter than the hypotenuse.
Example 1 · Find the hypotenuse

The legs are 4 and 7. Find c. Round to the nearest tenth.

  • Write: 4² + 7² = c²
  • Square: 16 + 49 = c², so 65 = c²
  • Square root: c = √65 ≈ 8.06…
  • The next digit is 6 (5 or more), so round up: c ≈ 8.1
Example 2 · Find a missing leg

One leg is 7. The hypotenuse is 12. Find the other leg, b. Round to the nearest tenth.

  • Write: 7² + b² = 12²
  • Square: 49 + b² = 144
  • Finding a leg means SUBTRACT: b² = 144 − 49 = 95
  • Square root: b = √95 ≈ 9.74…, so b ≈ 9.7

Check: 9.7 is shorter than 12 ✓

Try it · Find a side

Pick what to find. Move the sliders. Watch each step.

Skills you need
  • Decide: hypotenuse (add) or leg (subtract).
  • Square each number before you add or subtract.
  • Take the square root at the end.
  • Round to the nearest tenth.
Common mistakes
  • Mistake: Adding the sides without squaring (4 + 7 = 11).Fix: Square first: 16 + 49 = 65.
  • Mistake: Adding the squares to find a leg.Fix: For a leg, subtract: c² − b².
  • Mistake: Stopping at c² = 65.Fix: Take the square root: √65 ≈ 8.1.

The Converse: Is It a Right Triangle?

Big idea

Three sides make a right triangle only if the two short sides squared add up to the long side squared. Do the math. Your eyes can’t tell.

Words to know

ConverseThe theorem turned around. If a² + b² = c², then the triangle is a right triangle.
Longest sideAlways use it as c. Test the two shorter sides against it.
=?Means “is it equal?” Work out both sides, then compare.
Pythagorean tripleThree whole numbers that work, like 3, 4, 5 or 5, 12, 13.

How to test three sides

  • Put the sides in order. The longest side is c.
  • Square the two shorter sides and add them.
  • Square the longest side.
  • Same answer? Yes, it is a right triangle. Different? No, it is not.
Triples to know: 3, 4, 5 · 6, 8, 10 · 5, 12, 13 · 8, 15, 17 · 7, 24, 25 · 9, 12, 15
Example 1 · Sides 9, 12, 15

Is a triangle with sides 9, 12 and 15 a right triangle?

  • The longest side is 15, so c = 15.
  • Test: 9² + 12² =? 15²
  • Square: 81 + 144 =? 225
  • 225 = 225. Equal, so yes, it is a right triangle.

The right angle is across from the longest side.

Example 2 · Sides 7, 9, 12

Is a triangle with sides 7, 9 and 12 a right triangle?

  • The longest side is 12, so c = 12.
  • Test: 7² + 9² =? 12²
  • Square: 49 + 81 =? 144
  • 130 ≠ 144. Not equal, so no, it is not a right triangle.
Try it · Test three sides

Move the sliders or tap a set. The tool puts the sides in order and runs the test.

Skills you need
  • Find the longest side. Call it c.
  • Square and add the two shorter sides.
  • Compare with c². Equal means right.
  • Explain your answer with the numbers.
Common mistakes
  • Mistake: Using the sides in the order they are given.Fix: Sort first. The longest side is always c.
  • Mistake: Adding the sides without squaring.Fix: Square each side, then add.
  • Mistake: Saying “it looks like a right angle.”Fix: Do the test. 11, 13, 17 looks right, but 290 ≠ 289.
Part 8 · NC.8.G.7 · Unit 5

Word Problems with a Labeled Diagram

Big idea

Many real things make right triangles: a ladder and a wall, a ramp, a path across a rectangle. Draw it, label it, then use a² + b² = c².

Words to know

DiagramA quick drawing of the problem. Put every number on it.
DiagonalA line from one corner of a rectangle to the opposite corner.
Horizontal / verticalFlat, like the ground / straight up, like a wall.
Straight-line distanceThe shortest path between two places. It is the hypotenuse.
SituationLegs (a, b)Hypotenuse (c)
Ladder on a wallGround to the wall, height up the wallThe ladder
RampFlat distance, rise (how high)The ramp
Rectangle (TV, field, garden)Length, widthThe diagonal
Walk east, then northEast distance, north distanceThe straight path back
Steps: (1) Read. (2) Draw and label. (3) Find the right angle. Which side is c? (4) Write a² + b² = c². (5) Solve. Write the units.
Example 1 · Ladder

A 15-foot ladder leans against a wall. The bottom is 9 feet from the wall. How high up the wall does it reach?

  • The wall and ground make a right angle. The ladder is across from it, so the ladder is c = 15.
  • Write: 9² + h² = 15²
  • Square: 81 + h² = 225. Subtract: h² = 144
  • Square root: h = 12 feet
Example 2 · Shortcut across a garden

A garden is a rectangle, 15 m long and 8 m wide. A path goes from one corner to the opposite corner. How long is the path?

  • The path is the diagonal. It is the hypotenuse.
  • Write: 8² + 15² = c²
  • Square: 64 + 225 = 289
  • Square root: c = 17 m
Try it · Slide the ladder

A 15-foot ladder leans on a wall. Move the bottom away from the wall. What happens to the height?

Skills you need
  • Draw a diagram and put every number on it.
  • Find the right angle. The side across from it is c.
  • Choose: add (find c) or subtract (find a leg).
  • Answer the question that was asked, with units.
Common mistakes
  • Mistake: Calling the ladder a leg.Fix: The ladder is the slanted side. It is c.
  • Mistake: Answering a different question.Fix: Reread. “How much shorter?” needs one more step.
  • Mistake: Leaving off the units.Fix: Write feet, meters, inches or blocks.
Part 9 · NC.8.G.8 · Unit 5

Distance Between Two Points on a Grid

Big idea

To find the distance between two points, make a right triangle. The across and up distances are the legs. The distance is the hypotenuse.

Words to know

Coordinates (x, y)Where a point is. x = across. y = up or down.
Horizontal distanceHow far across. Subtract the x’s. We write Δx (“change in x”).
Vertical distanceHow far up or down. Subtract the y’s. We write Δy.
DistanceThe straight segment between the points. It is never negative.
  • Plot the two points. Draw a right triangle with a flat leg and a straight-up leg.
  • Across: Δx = bigger x − smaller x.
  • Up or down: Δy = bigger y − smaller y.
  • Use Δx² + Δy² = d². Take the square root. Round if needed.
Watch your signs: Subtracting a negative means adding. 5 − (−3) = 5 + 3 = 8, not 2. You can also count the boxes on the grid to check.
Example 1 · (1, 1) to (9, 7)

Find the distance between (1, 1) and (9, 7).

  • Across: Δx = 9 − 1 = 8
  • Up: Δy = 7 − 1 = 6
  • 8² + 6² = d², so 64 + 36 = 100
  • Square root: d = 10
Example 2 · With negatives

Find the distance between (−2, 3) and (3, −1). Round to the nearest tenth.

  • Across: Δx = 3 − (−2) = 3 + 2 = 5
  • Down: Δy = 3 − (−1) = 3 + 1 = 4
  • 5² + 4² = d², so 25 + 16 = 41
  • Square root: d = √41 ≈ 6.40…, so d ≈ 6.4
Try it · Move point B

Tap the grid to move point B, or use the sliders. A stays at (−3, −2). Watch the legs and the distance.

Skills you need
  • Plot points and draw the right triangle.
  • Find Δx and Δy. Subtract carefully with negatives.
  • Use a² + b² = c² with the legs Δx and Δy.
  • Take the square root and round.
Common mistakes
  • Mistake: 2 − (−4) = −2.Fix: Subtracting a negative means adding: 2 + 4 = 6.
  • Mistake: Adding Δx + Δy for the distance.Fix: Square them first, add, then take the square root.
  • Mistake: Giving a negative distance.Fix: Use bigger − smaller. Distance is never negative.
