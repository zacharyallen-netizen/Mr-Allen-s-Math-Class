2 Perfect Squares & Square Roots

📌 THE RELATIONSHIP: Squaring and square-rooting are INVERSE OPERATIONS — they undo each other. If 5² = 25, then √25 = 5. Know one, know the other!

A perfect square is a whole number times itself (n × n = n²). Its square root takes you back to that whole number. So √(a perfect square) is always a whole number.

Perfect squares are numbers of tiles that make a perfect square. The side is the square root: √16 = 4.

3 Perfect Cubes & Cube Roots

📌 THE RELATIONSHIP: Cubing and cube-rooting are INVERSE OPERATIONS too. If 5³ = 125, then ∛125 = 5. The cube-root symbol carries a small 3: ∛.

A perfect cube is a whole number used as a factor three times (n × n × n = n³). Its cube root undoes that.

2 × 2 × 2 = 8 small cubes, so 2³ = 8 and ∛8 = 2. The edge is the cube root.

4 Estimating Square Roots

📌 THE BIG IDEA: When a number is NOT a perfect square, its square root is irrational — it never ends or repeats. We can't write it exactly, but we can estimate it. Two tools: a number line, and a step-by-step algorithm.

Method 1 — Place it on a Number Line

Great for "between which two whole numbers?" questions.

  1. Find the two perfect squares your number lands between.
  2. Take the √ of each — those are two back-to-back whole numbers. Your root lands between them.
  3. Decide which one it's closer to: is your number nearer the lower or the upper perfect square?
  4. Place it on the number line.

Example: place √20

16 < 20 < 25, so √20 is between 4 and 5. Since 20 is closer to 16, √20 sits a little below the middle — closer to 4.

16 < 20 < 25, so √20 is between 4 and 5. (Calculator: 4.47…)

Method 2 — The Estimating Algorithm (to the nearest tenth)

How far is your number into the gap?

Between the two perfect squares, find how far along your number sits. That fraction, added to the smaller root, is your estimate. Watch the colors: gold = perfect squares, green = the roots, blue = your number.

Step 1 — Trap your number between two perfect squares.
36  <  45  <  49
√36 = 6  ·  √49 = 7  →  so √45 is between 6 and 7. The smaller root is 6.
Step 2 — Build the fraction: how far in is your number?
your number − lower square 45 − 36 = 9 49 − 36 = 13 upper square − lower square
= 9 ÷ 13 ≈ 0.7

The fraction is always squares on top and bottom — never the roots.

Step 3 — Add the fraction to the smaller root.
√45 ≈ 6 + 0.7 = 6.7

Calculator check: √45 = 6.708… ✓

45 is 9 of the 13 steps from 36 to 49, so √45 is about 9 ÷ 13 ≈ 0.7 of the way from 6 to 7.

💡 IN SHORT: √n ≈ (smaller root) + (n − lower square) ÷ (upper square − lower square). This is an estimate — it can be off by about a tenth from a calculator, and that's fine.
⚠ DON'T MIX THEM UP: the number you add is a root (6). The numbers inside the fraction are the perfect squares (36 and 49). Roots to add, squares to subtract.
CALC Estimate √30 to the nearest tenth using the algorithm. (Hint: 25 < 30 < 36)

Use the two gaps to build your fraction, then add it to 5.

25 < 30 < 36 → roots 5 and 6. Fraction = (30 − 25) ÷ (36 − 25) = 5 ÷ 11 ≈ 0.45. So √30 ≈ 5 + 0.45 = 5.45 → 5.5. (Calculator: 5.477…)

Negative Square Roots

📌 JUST FLIP THE SIGN: a negative in front puts your estimate on the other side of zero. Estimate the positive root first, then make it negative. Example: −√40 → √40 ≈ 6.3, so −√40 ≈ −6.3.

Same distance from 0, opposite sides: √40 ≈ 6.3 and −√40 ≈ −6.3.

🧭 WHICH METHOD? Use the number line for "between which whole numbers." Use the algorithm when you need a decimal to the nearest tenth. Both start the same way: find the two perfect squares that surround your number.

ERROR ANALYSIS To order 1.5, √2, 7/4, √3, a student wrote 1.5 < √2 < √3 < 7/4. Are they right?
Convert first: 1.5 = 1.5, √2 ≈ 1.41, 7/4 = 1.75, √3 ≈ 1.73. The student forgot that √2 ≈ 1.41 is the smallest. Correct order: √2 < 1.5 < √3 < 7/4.

Follow the student’s order. Least to greatest should always hop right; their first hop goes back.
