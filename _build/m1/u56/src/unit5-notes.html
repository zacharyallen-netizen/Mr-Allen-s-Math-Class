1 Representing Data

📌 A histogram shows the shape of one variable. A box plot shows the 5-number summary: min, Q1, median, Q3, max.
New words. Data is a list of numbers we collect. A dot plot puts one dot for each value above a number line. Dots for the same value stack up. A histogram uses bars. Each bar counts the values in one interval (a range, like 10–19). The bars touch. Frequency means “how many.” Quartiles are the three numbers that cut ordered data into four equal parts: Q1, the median, and Q3.

Worked example: Quiz scores 7, 4, 9, 5, 7, 10, 6, 8, 5, 7. Find the five-number summary.

1. Put the data in order: click to reveal 4, 5, 5, 6, 7, 7, 7, 8, 9, 10
2. Min and max (smallest and largest): click to reveal min = 4, max = 10
3. Median (10 values, so average the 5th and 6th): click to reveal (7 + 7) ÷ 2 = 7
4. Q1 = middle of the lower half 4, 5, 5, 6, 7: click to reveal Q1 = 5
✓ 5. Q3 = middle of the upper half 7, 7, 8, 9, 10: click to reveal Q3 = 8 → summary 4, 5, 7, 8, 10

Worked example: Ages at a party: 12, 15, 18, 21, 22, 25, 27, 28, 33, 36, 38, 41. Make a histogram.

1. Pick equal intervals: click to reveal 10–19, 20–29, 30–39, 40–49
2. Count the values in each interval: click to reveal 3, 5, 3, 1
✓ 3. Draw touching bars that tall. The tallest bar is: click to reveal 20–29 (5 people)

How to find the five-number summary

  1. Put the numbers in order from least to greatest.
  2. Write the min (first) and the max (last).
  3. Find the median. Odd count: the middle value. Even count: average the two middle values.
  4. Split the data into a lower half and an upper half. If the count is odd, leave the median out.
  5. Q1 is the median of the lower half. Q3 is the median of the upper half.
  6. Draw the box from Q1 to Q3. Draw a line at the median. Draw whiskers out to the min and max.

2 Center & Spread

📌 Center: mean (average) or median (middle). Spread: range, IQR = Q3 − Q1, and standard deviation (typical distance from the mean). Median & IQR resist outliers.
New words. The mean is the fair-share value: add all values, then divide by how many. It is the balance point of a dot plot. Spread tells how far apart the data are. The range is max − min. The MAD (mean absolute deviation) is the average distance from the mean. The standard deviation is another “typical distance” from the mean. Bigger MAD or standard deviation = more spread out.

Data: 3, 5, 7, 9, 16

Mean = (3+5+7+9+16) ÷ 5 = 40 ÷ 5 = click to reveal 8
Median (middle value): click to reveal 7
✓ Range = 16 − 3 = click to reveal 13

Worked example: Find the MAD of 3, 5, 7, 9, 16.

1. Find the mean: click to reveal 8 (from above)
2. Find each distance from 8 (always positive): click to reveal 5, 3, 1, 1, 8
3. Add the distances: click to reveal 5 + 3 + 1 + 1 + 8 = 18
✓ 4. Divide by how many values: click to reveal MAD = 18 ÷ 5 = 3.6

Worked example: Picture the standard deviation of 3, 5, 7, 9, 16.

1. Distances from the mean 8: click to reveal 5, 3, 1, 1, 8
2. Square each distance (the area of each square): click to reveal 25, 9, 1, 1, 64
3. Find the mean of the squares: click to reveal 100 ÷ 5 = 20
✓ 4. Take the square root: click to reveal standard deviation = √20 ≈ 4.47

On a calculator this is σx (sigma x). You usually let the calculator do it — the picture shows what it means.

How to find the MAD

  1. Find the mean.
  2. Find how far each value is from the mean. Distances are never negative.
  3. Add all the distances.
  4. Divide by how many values there are.

3 Outliers

📌 A value is an outlier if it is below Q1 − 1.5·IQR or above Q3 + 1.5·IQR. Outliers pull the mean, not the median.
Because the median resists outliers, it’s often the fairer ‘typical’ value for skewed data (like income).
New words. An outlier is a value far away from the rest. A fence is a cut-off line. Values past a fence are outliers. Skewed data has a long tail on one side. Resist means “does not move much.”

Worked example: Is 25 an outlier in 4, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 25?

1. Quartiles (8 values; halves 4, 6, 7, 8 and 9, 10, 11, 25): click to reveal Q1 = 6.5, Q3 = 10.5
2. IQR = Q3 − Q1: click to reveal 10.5 − 6.5 = 4
3. 1.5 × IQR: click to reveal 1.5 × 4 = 6
4. Fences: Q1 − 6 and Q3 + 6: click to reveal 0.5 and 16.5
✓ 5. Compare 25 to the upper fence: click to reveal 25 > 16.5, so 25 IS an outlier

How to test for outliers (1.5 × IQR rule)

  1. Find Q1 and Q3.
  2. Find IQR = Q3 − Q1.
  3. Multiply: 1.5 × IQR.
  4. Lower fence = Q1 − 1.5 × IQR. Upper fence = Q3 + 1.5 × IQR.
  5. Any value below the lower fence or above the upper fence is an outlier.

4 Scatter Plots & Regression

📌 The line of best fit ŷ = a + bx models the trend. Slope = predicted change per unit; intercept = predicted value at x = 0. Interpolate inside the data; extrapolate beyond it (risky).
New words. A scatter plot shows pairs of numbers (x, y) as dots. The line of best fit (or regression line) runs through the middle of the dots. ŷ is read “y-hat.” It means the predicted y. To interpolate is to predict for an x inside the data. To extrapolate is to predict for an x outside the data. That is risky.

Try it: move the sliders. Make the gold misses (residuals) as small as you can.

Model ŷ = 2 + 3x. Predict y when x = 4.

Substitute x = 4: 2 + 3(4) = click to reveal 2 + 12
✓ Predicted ŷ = click to reveal 14

1. Slope b = 1: click to reveal each extra hour of practice predicts about 1 more basket
2. Intercept a = 2: click to reveal with 0 hours of practice, the model predicts 2 baskets

How to predict with a model

  1. Find the x-value in the question.
  2. Put it in for x. Use parentheses: ŷ = 2 + 3(4).
  3. Multiply first. Then add.
  4. Check: is the x inside the data? If not, say the prediction is risky.

5 Residuals & Correlation

📌 Residual = actual − predicted. A good fit has small residuals scattered randomly. Correlation r is from −1 to 1: near ±1 = strong, near 0 = weak; the sign gives the direction.
New words. A residual is the miss: actual y minus predicted ŷ. Positive = the dot is above the line. Negative = below. A residual plot graphs the residuals. The correlation coefficient r is one number that tells the direction (+ or −) and the strength of a straight-line pattern.

Each r above is computed from that plot’s own dots.

Actual y = 14, predicted ŷ = 12. Find the residual.

✓ Residual = actual − predicted = 14 − 12 = click to reveal +2

Worked example: The basket data has r ≈ 0.94. Describe the correlation.

1. Sign of r: click to reveal positive → as x goes up, y tends to go up
2. Size of r (0.94 is close to 1): click to reveal strong
✓ 3. Describe it: click to reveal a strong positive linear correlation

Try it: drag a dot. Watch the line and r change.

How to find and read a residual

  1. Find the actual y (the dot).
  2. Find the predicted ŷ (plug x into the model).
  3. Subtract: actual − predicted.
  4. Positive → dot above the line. Negative → dot below the line.

6 Correlation vs. Causation

⚠️ Correlation is NOT causation. A strong relationship doesn’t prove one variable causes the other — a hidden lurking variable may explain both.
Ice-cream sales and shark attacks rise together — but hot weather drives both. Neither causes the other.
New words. Causation means one thing makes the other happen. A lurking variable is a hidden third thing that pushes both variables. Only a controlled experiment (we change one thing on purpose and keep the rest the same) can show cause.

Worked example: Students who sleep more tend to have higher grades (r = 0.6). Does more sleep cause higher grades?

1. Is there a correlation? click to reveal Yes — moderate and positive (r = 0.6)
2. Was it a controlled experiment? click to reveal No — the data was only observed
3. Name a possible lurking variable: click to reveal study habits (or less screen time) may raise both
✓ 4. Conclusion: click to reveal sleep and grades are related — we cannot say sleep causes the grades

How to judge a cause claim

  1. Check r: is there a real correlation?
  2. Ask: was it a controlled experiment?
  3. If not, think of a lurking variable that could push both.
  4. Say “is related to,” not “causes.”
