Unit 8 Guided Notes — Statistics

Use random samples to make predictions, and compare two groups with centers and spreads.

1 Populations and Samples NC.7.SP.1

📖 Words to know
Population: the WHOLE group.
Sample: part of the group.
Random sample: everyone has an equal chance to be picked.
Biased: leans one way; not fair.
📌 THE BIG IDEA: We use a sample to learn about a population when we cannot ask everyone. A random sample that is big enough usually represents the population well. A biased sample does not.
Question: favorite school lunch?SampleFair or biased?
Ask 40 names drawn from a hat of all studentsrandom?
Ask the first 40 students in the pizza lineconvenience?
Ask the 10 members of the cooking clubsmall, one group?

Example: a town wants to know if people want a new park. Which sample is best?

1Asking people AT the old park leans toward park lovers: biased.
2Asking 5 neighbors is too small.
3Best: pick 200 addresses at random from the whole town list.
⚠ Watch out: A big sample can still be biased. Asking 500 people at a soccer game about favorite sports leans toward soccer.
A school has 600 students. Ms. Lee asks 50 of them. How many students are in the POPULATION?
The population is all 600 students. The 50 are the sample.
Which sample is random?
A computer picking from ALL students gives everyone the same chance.
A survey about bedtimes asks 200 students at a late-night school dance. Why is it biased?
Students at a late dance lean toward later bedtimes, so the sample is not like the population.

2 Making Predictions from a Sample NC.7.SP.2

📖 Words to know
Inference: a conclusion about the population from a sample.
📌 THE BIG IDEA: Find the fraction (or percent) in the sample. Multiply it by the size of the population. Different random samples give a little different answers, so a prediction is an estimate.

12 of 50 sampled students walk to school. That is ?%.

For all 600 students: 0.24 × 600 = ? walkers (about).

Example: 9 of 40 light bulbs in a sample are broken. About how many of 800 bulbs are broken?

1Sample fraction: 9/40 = 0.225
20.225 × 800 = 180
3About 180 broken bulbs
⚠ Watch out: Use the sample fraction, then scale up to the WHOLE population, not to the number you have not asked.
In a random sample, 18 of 60 students like art best. About how many of 450 students like art best?
0.3 × 450 = 135.
Two random samples of 50 students each: 20 and 24 like rap. What is the best estimate of the percent of all students who like rap?
44 of 100 = 44%. It is an estimate, so "about".
In a random sample of 75 fish, 15 are trout. A lake has about 900 fish. How many trout should you expect?
0.2 × 900 = 180 trout.

3 Center and Spread NC.7.SP.4

📖 Words to know
Mean: add, then divide by how many.
Median: the middle when in order.
IQR: Q3 − Q1.
MAD: average distance from the mean.
📌 THE BIG IDEA: Centers: mean and median. Spreads: range, IQR and MAD. Use mean and MAD when the data is balanced. Use median and IQR when the data is lopsided or has an outlier.
11 students: 2, 4, 4, 5, 6, 6, 6, 7, 8, 9, 9.

Mean: 66 ÷ 11 = ?

Median (6th value): ?

Range: 9 − 2 = ?

Q1 = 4, Q3 = 8, IQR = ?

Example: find the MAD of 3, 5, 6, 8, 8

1Mean: 30 ÷ 5 = 6
2Distances from 6: 3, 1, 0, 2, 2
3Average distance: 8 ÷ 5 = 1.6
4MAD = 1.6
⚠ Watch out: Put the data in order before you find the median or quartiles.
Find the mean of 7, 9, 10, 10, 14.
50 ÷ 5 = 10.
Find the median of 12, 3, 20, 9, 21, 8.
(9 + 12) ÷ 2 = 10.5.
Find the MAD of 4, 6, 8, 10.
Mean = 7. Distances add to 8. 8 ÷ 4 = 2.

4 Comparing Two Data Sets NC.7.SP.3

📖 Words to know
Overlap: how much the two groups share the same values.
Difference of means in MADs: (difference of the means) ÷ MAD. Bigger = less overlap.
📌 THE BIG IDEA: To compare two groups, look at the difference of the centers and the spread. If the centers are far apart compared with the spread (2 or more MADs), the groups are really different.
Mean of A = 6. Mean of B = 10. Both MADs ≈ 0.9. The means are about 4.5 MADs apart: very little overlap.

Difference of means: 10 − 6 = ?

The MAD is about 0.9, so the means are about ? MADs apart.

Example: two classes' test scores have means 80 and 86. Both MADs are 3. How far apart are the means?

186 − 80 = 6
26 ÷ 3 = 2
32 MADs apart: a clear difference, but some overlap.
⚠ Watch out: A difference in means means more when the spread is SMALL. A 5-point difference is big if MAD = 1, and small if MAD = 10.
Group means are 14 and 20. Both MADs are 2. How many MADs apart are the means?
6 ÷ 2 = 3 MADs.
Which pair of groups overlaps the MOST?
2 ÷ 8 = 0.25 MAD apart: lots of overlap.
Box plots: Team A median 18, IQR 6. Team B median 24, IQR 6. Which is the best statement?
Median B (24) > median A (18), a difference of 1 IQR. The plots overlap a lot.

5 Comparing Populations with Samples NC.7.SP.4

📖 Words to know
Comparative inference: a conclusion that compares two populations using random samples from each.
📌 THE BIG IDEA: Take random samples from BOTH populations. Compare their centers (mean or median) and spreads. Say what is likely true, using the numbers.
7th graders8th graders
Sample size2020
Median sleep (hours)8.57.5
IQR (hours)11
Random samples of 20 from each grade.

Example: what can you say from the table?

1Medians: 8.5 vs 7.5, a 1-hour difference.
2IQRs are both 1, so the difference is 1 IQR.
37th graders likely sleep more than 8th graders, but many students in both grades sleep the same amount.
⚠ Watch out: Use words like "likely" or "usually". A sample cannot prove that EVERY member is the same.
Random samples: 6th grade median reading time 20 min; 7th grade median 32 min. How many more minutes is the 7th grade median?
32 − 20 = 12 minutes.
Two samples of plant heights: Sunny mean 30 cm, MAD 2. Shady mean 24 cm, MAD 2. Which inference is best?
30 − 24 = 6, which is 3 MADs. Sunny plants are likely taller.
Sample A: 15 of 75 students walk to school. The school has 900 students. Sample B (another school of 900): 27 of 75 walk. About how many MORE students walk at the second school?
A: 180. B: 324. 324 − 180 = 144 more.
